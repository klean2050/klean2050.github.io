Learning transferable biosignal representations

I develop compact learning methods for physiological signals. I want representations that preserve the information needed to study human function and remain useful across tasks, people, and recording conditions.

Long recordings contain many samples, but the information relevant to a particular process may lie in a few temporal, spectral, or spatial patterns. I design models around these properties, and study how their training objectives shape what they learn. This connects efficiency with generalization: I ask which aspects of the signal remain useful outside the training setting.

In WildECG, I trained a self-supervised state-space model on 275,000 ECG segments recorded in everyday settings. Its training task identifies transformations applied to a signal, encouraging sensitivity to distortions while the architecture efficiently models long sequences. [1]

I examine this question at the signal level with BioCodec, using quantization to turn continuous biosignals into discrete codes. This bottleneck learns compact signal structure: a tokenizer trained on single-channel EEG retained information useful for clinical, sleep, and neural-decoding tasks. [2]

I then used these codes to develop BrainCodes, a lightweight EEG foundation model over discrete neural codes, pretrained on recordings from over 1,000 participants. It matches or exceeds larger models on several evaluated tasks, with up to 97% fewer trainable parameters. [3]

To understand this transfer, I also examine the representations themselves. Predictive accuracy alone does not establish which aspects of physiology a model uses. My work examines generalization across recording settings [4] and bias toward aperiodic and low-frequency activity [5]. I also developed spatial and spectral probes to distinguish information encoded in a representation from information that influences its predictions [6].

I am interested in how reconstruction, contrastive, and predictive objectives preserve different aspects of physiology. I want to develop hierarchical representations that make both transient events and stable individual traits accessible, with evaluations that test signal morphology, oscillations, and spatial relationships.
